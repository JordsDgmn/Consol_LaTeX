# Recognition Memory for Syntactic and Semantic Aspects of Connected Discourse  
**JACQUELINE STRUNK SACHS**  
Bell Telephone Laboratories, Murray Hill, N.J.

---

This study investigates the pattern of retention of syntactic and semantic information shortly after comprehension of connected discourse. Ninety-six Ss listened to 24 taped passages and, after each passage, heard one recognition test sentence which was either identical to a sentence that had occurred in the passage, or was changed in some slight way. The Ss responded "identical" or "changed," rated their confidence, and classified changes as “meaning” or “form.”

Two independent variables were manipulated:

1. **The relationship between the original sentence and the test sentence:**
   - (a) Semantically changed  
   - (b) Active → passive or passive → active  
   - (c) Formally changed in ways that did not affect meaning  
   - (d) Unchanged  

2. **The amount of interpolated material** between the original and test sentences:  
   - 0 syllables  
   - 80 syllables  
   - 160 syllables  

Each S heard passages representing all levels of each variable. All combinations of particular passages, relationship conditions, and interpolated material were tested.

**Findings:**  
- When the test sentence followed immediately after the original, retention was high for all types.  
- After 80–160 syllables, *recognition for syntactic changes dropped to near chance*, while semantic recognition remained high.  
- Even when meaning was remembered, syntactic or formal properties were forgotten rapidly.

Results suggest that the original *surface structure* is stored only long enough for comprehension. Once a semantic interpretation is made, memory stores **meaning**, not form.

---

When language is comprehended, meaning is remembered to some extent; however, *exact wording* is forgotten unless special attention is given to style. This aligns with linguistic theory (Chomsky, 1965).

A naïve view might assume meaning is derived directly from the string of words, but syntactic structure plays a crucial role. For instance:

- “Wrestling monkeys are entertaining”  
- “Wrestling monkeys is entertaining”

These differ in meaning although they differ only in *“are”* vs *“is”*.

Even a single sentence can have multiple meanings depending on its deep structure:

- “Wrestling monkeys can be entertaining”  
   → could mean *it is entertaining to wrestle with monkeys*  
   → or *monkeys wrestling is entertaining*

Thus, comprehension depends on **syntactic structure** (deep structure), not just word meanings.

### Linguistic Model  
A sentence’s representation follows layers:

- **Sounds** → physical stimuli  
- **Surface structure** → perceived words/phrases  
- **Deep structure** → grammatical relations independent of surface order  

Memory of meaning depends on this deep structure.

According to the model:

- Characteristics of surface form that are *not required* for meaning are **not retained** after comprehension.  
- Thus, people often recall *meaning-equivalent paraphrases* rather than exact wordings.

This idea is supported by the long tradition of verbal memory research: wording is forgotten, while meaning remains.

The study explores **whether syntactic information is stored at all**, how long it persists, and whether some types of syntactic structure are better retained.

The experiment tests how well Ss can recognize syntactic changes that *do not* alter meaning.

---

The study requires a clear distinction between *semantic* and *syntactic* aspects. Because some syntactic differences do not change meaning, sentences can differ formally but share the same deep structure.

Examples of **formal (stylistic) changes**:

- “He looked the number up”  
- “He looked up the number”

Example of **semantic change**:

- Active vs passive may change semantic emphasis, depending on linguistic framework.

### Theoretical Hypothesis  
If memory stores only *meaning*, then:

- Ss should recognize **semantic changes** reliably  
- Ss should quickly forget **purely formal/syntactic changes**  

Sentences that differ only in form but not meaning should be difficult to recognize as different after only a brief interval.

# Background and Theoretical Motivation

Research in verbal memory traditionally distinguishes between:

- **Retention of meaning**, which is relatively durable  
- **Retention of surface form**, which is often lost rapidly unless rehearsal or special attention occurs  

Earlier studies (e.g., Johnson, Raye, & others) suggest that when people paraphrase sentences, their recall of *exact wording* deteriorates quickly, even after a few seconds. However, many such studies used **isolated sentences**, and did not test memory for syntactic structure within *connected discourse*.

Sachs notes that prior research has not fully addressed:

1. **How long the surface structure persists** after comprehension  
2. **Whether syntactic information is retained differently from semantic information**  
3. **How recognition memory behaves in continuous, meaningful discourse**, as opposed to unrelated sentences  

The present study fills this gap.

---

# Key Experimental Questions

The research addresses:

1. **Do people retain syntactic information after comprehending a sentence?**
2. **How does retention of syntactic information compare with retention of meaning?**
3. **How does retention vary as the interval between hearing and testing increases?**
4. **Do different types of syntactic transformations differ in difficulty?**

To answer these, Sachs uses carefully designed **recognition tests** inserted into connected prose passages.

---

# Method

## Subjects (Ss)
- **N = 96**  
- College-aged adults  
- Divided into groups counterbalanced across conditions

Each subject heard **24 passages** on tape, followed by **24 test sentences**, one per passage.

---

# Materials

### Passages
- Each passage was a short paragraph (2–3 sentences) of **connected English discourse**.  
- Content was neutral and simple, designed to minimize prior knowledge effects.  
- Each passage contained a **critical sentence** that would later be tested.

To avoid memory contamination:

- No passage repeated the same syntactic structure in a predictable way  
- No unusual or striking wording was used  
- Critical sentences were constructed to allow manipulation without changing meaning unless intended

---

# Construction of Test Sentences

For each critical sentence, four versions were prepared:

### **1. Identical Sentence**
The exact wording of the original.

### **2. Semantic Change**
Meaning was altered while preserving some structural similarity.

Example:
- Original: “The ants ate the sweet jelly.”
- Semantic change: “The ants found the sweet jelly.”

Here, the surface structure may remain similar, but **meaning differs**.

### **3. Syntactic Change in Voice (Active ↔ Passive)**
Meaning preserved as closely as possible.

Example:
- Original active: “The dog chased the cat.”  
- Passive test: “The cat was chased by the dog.”

These differ in form but (in most theories) **retain the same deep structure meaning**.

### **4. Pure Formal / Surface Change (stylistic, movement)**
Surface form changes but **deep structure remains identical**.

Example:
- “He looked the number up.”  
- “He looked up the number.”

Or:
- “The girl read the story to the boy.”  
- “The girl read to the boy the story.”

These are syntactic differences that **do not affect meaning**.

---

# Interpolated Material

To test how quickly form disappears from memory, Sachs manipulated the amount of **intervening discourse** between the original sentence and its recognition test.

Three conditions:

1. **0 syllables**  
   - Test sentence comes **immediately** after the critical sentence.

2. **80 syllables**  
   - Approximately 2–3 sentences before the test.

3. **160 syllables**  
   - About 4–6 sentences before the test.

These conditions allow measurement of **decay over time** in a naturalistic context.

---

# Procedure

### Step-by-step:
1. Subject listens to a recorded passage.  
2. After the passage ends, a **single test sentence** is played.  
3. The subject responds:
   - “**Identical**” or “**Changed**”
4. If “changed,” the subject classifies the change as:
   - **Meaning change**  
   - **Form change**
5. Subject rates **confidence** on a numerical scale.

Each subject goes through 24 such trials.

All versions of each manipulated condition are counterbalanced to avoid order effects.

---

# Example of Materials (from the paper)

### Original sentence:
“**The man got out of the car to fix a flat tire.**”

### Possible manipulations:
- **Semantic change**: “The man got out of the car to check the engine.”  
- **Syntactic voice change**: “To fix a flat tire, the man got out of the car.”  
- **Formal change**: “The man got out of the car in order to fix a flat tire.”

Subjects must decide whether the test is **identical** or **changed**, and what type of change it is.

---

# Experimental Design Summary

- Fully factorial across:
  - 4 relationship types (identical, semantic, syntactic, formal)
  - 3 delay intervals (0, 80, 160 syllables)
- 96 Ss each receiving all combinations  
- 24 passages × 4 versions counterbalanced across groups  
- Recognition judgment + classification + confidence rating

The design allows detailed comparison of **recognition accuracy** across conditions, giving access to how syntactic and semantic memory decay over short temporal intervals.
