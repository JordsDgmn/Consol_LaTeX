Learning is often considered complete when a student can produce the correct answer to a question. In our research, students in one condition learned foreign-language vocabulary words in the standard paradigm of repeated study–test trials. In three other conditions, once a student had correctly produced the vocabulary item, it was:

- repeatedly **studied but dropped from further testing**,  
- repeatedly **tested but dropped from further study**, or  
- **dropped from both study and test**.

Repeated studying after learning had **no effect** on delayed recall, but repeated testing produced a **large positive effect**. In addition, students’ predictions of their performance were **uncorrelated** with actual performance. The results demonstrate the critical role of **retrieval practice** in consolidating learning.

---

# Background and Motivation

Since Ebbinghaus (1885), most learning research assumes that:

- **Learning occurs during study**,  
- **Testing merely measures learning**, without contributing to it.

However, some prior work (Bjork, Gates, Izawa, Tulving) suggests that **retrieval itself** might cause learning.

Despite this, the belief remains widespread in education that tests are **neutral assessments** instead of **learning events**.

This paper directly tests:

1. Whether study repetition improves retention after information is already recalled.  
2. Whether test repetition (retrieval practice) improves long-term learning.  
3. Whether students understand what actually helps them learn.

---

# Research Questions

1. After an item is successfully recalled once, what produces more long-term learning?
   - More **study**, or  
   - More **testing** (retrieval)?

2. How accurate are students’ predictions of learning?

3. Do quick-to-learn items enjoy better long-term retention?

The authors evaluate these classic assumptions using a controlled vocabulary-learning experiment.

---

# Experimental Design

Subjects learned **40 Swahili–English vocabulary pairs**, e.g.:

- **mashua – boat**

The design began with:

- A **study** period, then  
- A **test** period (recall: mashua → ?)

All conditions were identical **until the first successful recall** of each item.

After an item was recalled once, it was treated in four different ways:

---

## **1. ST (Standard Study-Test Condition)**  
- Continue to **study** the item in every study period  
- Continue to **test** it in every test period  

## **2. SNT (Study Nonrecalled Items + Test All Items)**  
- If recalled: **removed from study**  
- Still **tested** in every test period  

## **3. STN (Study All Items + Test Nonrecalled Items)**  
- If recalled: **removed from testing**  
- Still **restudied** each study period  

## **4. SNTN (Drop from both Study and Test after first recall)**  
- Once recalled: **never studied again**, **never tested again**  

This fourth condition reflects common “study until you know it, then drop it” advice.

---

# Students’ Task Flow

1. Study 40 vocabulary pairs  
2. Test on all pairs (recall English from Swahili cue)  
3. Continue through repeated study & test periods  
4. After each first successful recall, apply the condition rules  
5. After learning, make a **prediction**:
   - “How many items will you remember in one week?”  
6. Return **one week later** for a delayed final recall test

---

# Results

## **1. Learning Curves: All Conditions Learned Equally Well**

Figure 1 (described):

- Cumulative learning (first successful recall) was nearly **identical** across conditions.
- All 40 items were learned by nearly all students.

Thus, **learning speed was the same** for all four conditions.

---

## **2. Students’ Predictions: All Groups Predicted ~50% Recall**

Average predicted recall out of 40 items:

- ST = 20.8  
- SNT = 20.4  
- STN = 22.0  
- SNTN = 20.3  

Students believed all conditions would yield similar retention.

But…

---

## **3. One-Week Delayed Recall: Massive Differences**

Actual recall after 1 week:

- **ST** (repeated study + test): ~80%  
- **SNT** (test only): ~80%  
- **STN** (study only): ~36%  
- **SNTN** (drop after recall): ~33%

### Key Findings:

- **Repeated testing** → huge retention benefit  
- **Repeated studying** → *no measurable benefit*  
- Dropping items from testing → catastrophic forgetting  

Effect size of retrieval practice:

\[
d = 4.03
\]

There was **zero overlap** between distributions of tested vs. non-tested conditions.

---

# Interpretation

### **Retrieval practice creates learning**  
Testing is *not* neutral—it is a powerful learning event.

### **Studying without retrieval does not strengthen long-term memory**  
Once an item is successfully recalled:

- Extra studying is **wasted effort**  
- Extra testing is **highly beneficial**

### **Students are unaware of this**  
Their predictions failed to match their actual performance.

---

# Practical Implications

- Students should **continue self-testing**, even after they can recall items.  
- “Study until learned, then drop” = incorrect strategy  
- Retrieval practice is essential for:
  - Long-term retention  
  - Consolidating learning  
  - Strengthening memory traces

The authors show that retrieval—not encoding repetition—produces durable learning.
