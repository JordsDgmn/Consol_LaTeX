# ABSTRACT
“Gamified” applications and services aim to provide game-like experiences. They usually do so by using game design elements in non-game contexts. This paper seeks to define “gamification” by using a conceptual analysis of the term as it has become popular in industry and academia. We propose a definition of gamification as the use of game design elements in non-game contexts. We distinguish gamification from similar concepts such as serious games, playful design, persuasive technology, and others.

# INTRODUCTION
Recently, we have seen a rapid rise in the popularity and ubiquity of what industry and practitioners term **“gamification.”** Many applications, platforms, and services have begun implementing game-like features or templates to encourage user engagement, loyalty, and participation.

The popular press has introduced the concept with headlines like “Gamification: The new way of motivating people,” “Why gamification is the future,” and “How companies are turning work into play.” Industry analysts predict a strong uptake of such systems.

However, despite widespread usage, the term “gamification” currently suffers from vague meanings and conceptual confusion. Different companies and authors define it inconsistently. Because of the lack of a solid conceptual framework, both academic debate and practitioner implementation struggle with unclear scope, shared vocabulary, and inconsistent evaluation.

This paper tries to clarify the term *from a conceptual standpoint*. Our intention is to:

1. Offer a clear, rigorous definition.  
2. Situate gamification relative to neighboring concepts such as:  
   - playful interaction  
   - serious games  
   - toys  
   - game-based technologies  
   - persuasive design  
3. Provide a vocabulary that supports design, analysis, and research.

We argue that **gamification is best understood as:**

> **“The use of game design elements in non-game contexts.”**

This definition allows us to distinguish gamification from full-fledged games, toys, playful design, and other adjacent fields by analyzing the underlying components being used and the context in which they are applied.

---

# THE PROBLEM OF DEFINING “GAMIFICATION”
The rise of the term began around 2010 in industry circles. Companies started adding points, badges, and leaderboards to websites and services. While practitioners promoted the term enthusiastically, academic discussions lagged behind. Definitions differed widely across blogs, whitepapers, and conference talks.

For example:

- Some defined gamification as “the use of game mechanics in non-game contexts.”  
- Others saw it as “applying game thinking to solve problems and engage audiences.”  
- Others broadened it to “turning systems into games.”

Such variability makes comparison between projects and research difficult. A robust definition requires examining the underlying components of games and identifying which components gamification borrows.

To address this, the authors use a **conceptual analysis** approach. They break down:

- What *games* consist of (rules, goals, feedback systems, interfaces, etc.)  
- What *design elements* constitute games  
- What *contexts* are considered “non-game”  
- What *kinds* of experiences gamification aims to produce (e.g., “gamefulness” vs. “playfulness”)

---

# RELATED TERMINOLOGY: GAMES, PLAY, GAMEFULNESS, PLAYFULNESS
To define gamification, the authors first clarify foundational distinctions in game studies.

## Games vs. Play
A widely accepted distinction in game studies is:

### **Play**
- Free-form  
- Open-ended  
- Often improvisational  
- Lacks structured goals  
- Associated with exploration and spontaneity

### **Game**
- Rule-based  
- Goal-oriented  
- Structured with constraints  
- Produces measurable outcomes (scores, win states, etc.)

The terms overlap, but they are not identical.

## Gamefulness vs. Playfulness
The authors extend the game–play distinction into a **gamefulness–playfulness** distinction:

### *Playfulness*
- Creative  
- Expressive  
- Open-ended  
- A stance or style of interaction

### *Gamefulness*
- Structured  
- Goal-driven  
- Competitive or performance-oriented  
- Derived from the emotional/experiential qualities of games

“Gamification,” then, is specifically concerned with **gamefulness**, not playfulness. Gamified systems attempt to create experiences resembling *games* (structured, goal-oriented), not *play* (free exploration).

## Application to Gamification
Gamification is:

- **Not** simply about making something “fun.”  
- **Not** about creating open-ended play spaces.  
- Rather, it is about **importing formal qualities of game design** (e.g., goals, rules, feedback) into **non-game contexts** to encourage *gameful* experiences.

Examples include:
- Points systems  
- Achievement badges  
- Leveling and progression  
- Leaderboards  
- Quests or challenges  
- Structured feedback loops

# WHY DEFINE GAMIFICATION VIA GAME DESIGN ELEMENTS?
Earlier definitions focused on “game mechanics,” “game thinking,” or “making work fun,” but these were imprecise.

The authors argue for **design elements** as the essential unit because:

1. **Games are complex systems** made of many overlapping components.  
2. Gamification rarely imports an entire game; it selectively uses parts.  
3. “Design elements” is broad enough to include:  
   - game mechanics  
   - interface design patterns  
   - conceptual models  
   - experiential design patterns  
4. This avoids debates about what counts as a “mechanic” vs. “dynamics” vs. “aesthetics.”

Thus, *design elements* is the most inclusive, precise category.

---

# TOWARD A TYPOLOGY OF GAME DESIGN ELEMENTS
The paper proposes a **hierarchy** of game-related design elements that can be used in gamification:

1. **Game interface design patterns**  
   (e.g., badges, points, leaderboards)

2. **Game design patterns and mechanics**  
   (e.g., turns, time constraints, resources)

3. **Game design principles and heuristics**  
   (e.g., clear goals, meaningful choices)

4. **Game models**  
   (e.g., MDA—Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics)

5. **Game design methodologies**  
   (e.g., iterative prototyping, playtesting cycles)

This typology helps distinguish between:
- shallow gamification (only adding UI elements)  
- deeper gamification (importing more structural design principles)  

---

# GAMES VS. GAMIFICATION
A **game**, by definition, is a full, rule-based system in which players voluntarily participate.

Gamification systems:

- Do **not** create full-fledged games  
- Do **not** constitute entire rule-based worlds  
- Instead, they **borrow and embed** certain game elements inside a non-game activity  

Examples:
- Foursquare badges for check-ins  
- Duolingo’s XP and streak systems  
- Fitbit step challenges  
- Khan Academy mastery points  

These are not “games,” but they leverage game design techniques to influence behavior.

---

# GAMIFICATION VS. SERIOUS GAMES
Serious games = full games designed for a purpose **beyond entertainment** (education, health, training).

Gamified systems:

- Are *not* full games  
- Use *fragments* of game design  
- Remain fundamentally *non-games* in purpose and structure  

Thus:

- A flight simulator → **serious game**  
- Adding a badge system to an e-learning platform → **gamification**

# GAMIFICATION VS. TOYS
Toys are artifacts that afford *play*—the free-form exploratory activity defined earlier. They do not enforce rules, goals, or quantifiable outcomes. Examples include:

- dolls  
- construction blocks  
- stuffed animals  
- sandbox environments  
- open-ended digital toys  

Gamification is fundamentally different because:

- It does **not** invite unstructured play.  
- It introduces rules, goals, and feedback systems.  
- It attempts to evoke **gamefulness**, not playfulness.  

Therefore, systems that simply allow playful exploration without importing game structure are *not* gamification.

---

# GAMIFICATION VS. PLAYFUL DESIGN
Playful design focuses on making interactions more open, expressive, and exploratory. These designs:

- celebrate ambiguity  
- use whimsical or humorous aesthetics  
- emphasize free engagement  
- aim to make systems *delightful* rather than goal-driven  

Gamification, by contrast:

- emphasizes progression  
- sets explicit goals  
- uses metrics, scores, and achievements  
- structures behavior  

As a result:

- A whimsical “easter egg” animation → **playful design**  
- A point-based reward for completing tasks → **gamification**

They share inspiration in games and play but differ in underlying philosophy.

---

# GAMIFICATION VS. PERSUASIVE TECHNOLOGY
Persuasive technology uses system design to influence user behavior (Fogg 2003). Common applications:

- encouraging exercise  
- promoting environmentally friendly actions  
- shaping consumer behavior  

At first glance, gamification is compatible with persuasive aims because game elements can motivate action.

However, the difference lies in **design lineage**:

- Persuasive technologies stem from **behavioral psychology and computing**.  
- Gamification stems from **game design and motivational game structures**.  

Gamification may serve persuasive goals, but not all persuasive systems employ game elements.

Thus:

- A step counter that sends reminders = persuasive technology  
- A step counter with points, badges, and leaderboards = persuasive technology **using gamification**

---

# GAMIFICATION VS. GAME-BASED TECHNOLOGIES
Game-based technologies include:

- game engines  
- game-like visualization techniques  
- graphical representations inspired by games  

These are **technological components**, not experiential structures.

Gamification, in contrast:

- uses design *elements*, not necessarily technologies  
- focuses on experience and behavior  
- may use simple UI widgets, not full engines  

Example:

- Using Unity to render a 3D simulation = game-based technology  
- Adding a badge reward for correct actions = gamification  

---

# NON-GAME CONTEXTS
A critical component of the definition is that game design elements are applied in **non-game** contexts. That is:

- Work  
- Education  
- Health  
- Productivity  
- Marketing  
- Social networks  

The authors emphasize that the *contextual frame* (Goffman, 1974) defines whether an activity is treated as a game.

If the system is framed and understood as a **game**, the activity is a game—even if its goal is serious.  
If the system uses game elements **within a non-game frame**, the activity is gamified.

Examples:

- Duolingo is framed as language learning (non-game) → gamified  
- Minecraft Education Edition is framed as a game you learn in → serious game

# WHAT COUNTS AS A “GAME DESIGN ELEMENT”?
The authors propose a **component hierarchy** to clarify what “game design elements” includes.

## 1. Game Interface Design Patterns
These are surface-level UI features commonly associated with games:

- points  
- badges  
- progress bars  
- leaderboards  
- virtual currencies  
- avatars  
- levels  
- health bars  

These have become widespread in software and are often the first elements copied into gamified systems.

## 2. Game Design Patterns and Mechanics
These are recurring solution formats found in game systems (Björk & Holopainen):

- time pressure  
- turns  
- resource management  
- randomized rewards  
- quests  
- challenges  
- win/loss conditions  
- collecting  
- combos  
- cooldowns  

These are deeper structural components than simple UI widgets.

## 3. Game Design Principles or Heuristics
General principles guiding good game design (e.g.):

- clear goals  
- meaningful choices  
- feedback loops  
- balance  
- pacing and difficulty curves  
- flow (Csikszentmihalyi)  

Gamified systems often borrow these high-level design ideas.

## 4. Game Models
Conceptual frameworks describing how games function:

- MDA (Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics)  
- Flow model  
- Bartle’s player types  
- Lazzaro’s “4 Keys to Fun”  

These can guide the creation of gamified experiences without copying specific patterns.

## 5. Game Design Methods
Industry-standard processes:

- iterative prototyping  
- playtesting  
- user-centered game design  
- rapid iteration cycles  

Even the **development methodology** of game design can be imported into non-game systems.

# DEFINING GAMIFICATION
Based on the conceptual analysis, the authors propose:

## **Definition:**
> **Gamification is the use of game design elements in non-game contexts.**

Breaking this down:

### “Use”
- Partial adoption, not full games  
- System incorporates game elements but remains fundamentally non-game  

### “Game Design Elements”
- Multiple layers: interface patterns, mechanics, patterns, models, methods  

### “Non-Game Contexts”
- Settings where the primary purpose is not play or entertainment  

This definition explicitly **excludes**:

- serious games  
- full educational games  
- simulations treated as games  
- playful design without structure  

---

# IMPLICATIONS OF THE DEFINITION
The definition allows researchers and designers to:

1. **Bound the field clearly**  
   Distinguish gamification from neighboring categories.

2. **Analyze design depth**  
   Is the system using superficial (points/badges) or deep elements (mechanics, principles)?

3. **Understand user experiences**  
   Gamification aims at **gameful** rather than playful experiences.

4. **Evaluate claims and outcomes**  
   Does the system produce desired motivation or behavior?  
   Is the gamification shallow or systemic?

5. **Avoid conceptual confusion**  
   Provides common ground for comparing systems.

---

# CRITIQUE OF CURRENT GAMIFICATION PRACTICE
The authors argue early gamification (2010–2011) was often shallow, consisting mostly of:

- badges  
- points  
- leaderboards  

This “PBL triad” represents:

- the simplest  
- least structural  
- least meaningful  
- most extrinsic  

form of game elements.

The authors caution that **mere pointification** or interface stickers may not replicate the motivational qualities of full games.

Deeper gamification should:

- consider intrinsic motivation  
- leverage meaningful challenges  
- incorporate feedback loops  
- support autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Self-Determination Theory)  
- use whole systems, not isolated widgets

# LIMITATIONS OF MISAPPLIED “GAMIFICATION”
A recurring concern in both academia and industry is that gamification is frequently reduced to light-weight reward “skins” placed atop existing systems. The authors highlight several potential problems with shallow gamification:

1. **Overreliance on extrinsic motivators**  
   Points, badges, and leaderboards (PBLs) primarily tap into *extrinsic* motivation.  
   As decades of motivational psychology show (Deci & Ryan, 1985; 2000), extrinsic rewards:

   - can crowd out intrinsic motivation  
   - may produce short-term compliance but long-term disengagement  
   - often fail when rewards are removed  

2. **Poor fit with user motivations**  
   Not all users are “Achievers.”  
   Some respond better to exploration, social connection, or creativity.  
   A narrow PBL focus fails to capture this diversity.

3. **Surface-level design**  
   Applying game elements without understanding the underlying systems of games results in:

   - lack of meaningful goals  
   - unbalanced feedback loops  
   - poor pacing or unclear challenge progression  

4. **Ethical concerns**  
   Gamification may push users toward behaviors they did not consciously choose.  
   The line between motivation and manipulation becomes unclear.

Thus, using game elements does not guarantee a gameful experience.  
Meaningful gamification requires deeper understanding of game design.

---

# GAMEFUL DESIGN VS. GAMIFICATION
The authors introduce an important distinction:

### **Gameful Design (broader context)**  
Designing for *gamefulness*, using game design insights without necessarily importing explicit game elements.

### **Gamification (narrower context)**  
Using *specific* game design elements within a non-game system.

Thus:

- Gameful Design = the “big picture” conceptual approach  
- Gamification = the “practical implementation” using particular elements

This distinction parallels:

- Playful Design vs. Play  
- Serious Games vs. Game-Inspired Applications  

The authors argue much of the industry uses “gamification” when they really mean “gameful design.”

---

# TOWARD A TAXONOMY OF GAMIFICATION
The authors propose that gamification must be analyzed along several axes:

1. **Depth of implementation**  
   - Surface (UI)  
   - Structural (mechanics, goals)  
   - Systemic (game model, feedback loops)  

2. **Type of experience targeted**  
   - Competitive  
   - Exploratory  
   - Social  
   - Mastery-oriented  

3. **Motivation model used**  
   - Extrinsic  
   - Intrinsic  
   - Mixed  

4. **Contextual framing**  
   - Education  
   - Work  
   - Health  
   - Productivity  
   - Marketing  

5. **User type considerations**  
   Drawing on:
   - Bartle types  
   - Lazzaro’s 4 Fun Keys  
   - SDT (autonomy, competence, relatedness)

A structured taxonomy helps differentiate trivial implementations from meaningful ones.

# GAMEFULNESS AND MOTIVATION (PAGE 15)
A central motivation for using gamification is its assumed ability to increase engagement and participation. The paper situates this assumption in relation to **Self-Determination Theory (SDT)** (Deci & Ryan, 2000), which distinguishes between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation.

Gamification tends to rely heavily on **extrinsic motivators**, such as:

- points  
- badges  
- rewards  
- levels  
- leaderboards  

These elements often strive to create an *appearance* of progress and achievement, encouraging users to continue behaving in ways valued by the system.

However, the authors warn that extrinsic motivators:

- **do not necessarily create intrinsic motivation**,  
- may undermine autonomy,  
- and may weaken long-term internal engagement.

In contrast, intrinsic motivators arise from:

- curiosity  
- mastery  
- challenge  
- competence  
- autonomy  
- purpose  

Good game design often supports intrinsic motivators through meaningful goals, interesting challenges, and well-paced feedback.  
**Superficial gamification may fail to replicate these conditions.**

# MISCONCEPTIONS ABOUT GAMIFICATION (PAGE 16)
The authors highlight several misconceptions:

### **1. “Gamification turns anything into a game.”**
False.  
Gamification imports *elements* of games; it does not transform a system into a full game.

### **2. “Gamification will always increase engagement.”**
False.  
Game elements may:

- bore users  
- frustrate users  
- manipulate them  
- fail to meaningfully connect to the user’s goals  

### **3. “Points and badges = gamification.”**
Partial and trivial.  
Gamification includes many deeper design elements beyond PBLs.

### **4. “Gamification is new.”**
Historically, point-based reward systems have existed for decades in:

- loyalty programs  
- educational behaviorism  
- workplace incentive programs  

The novelty is the **explicit borrowing from game design**.

# DESIGNING FOR GAMEFUL EXPERIENCES (PAGE 17)
To design meaningful gamification, one must understand:

### **1. What makes games engaging**
Games provide:

- clear goals  
- structured progression  
- meaningful challenges  
- actionable feedback  
- balanced pacing  
- voluntary participation  
- a frame of safety for experimentation  

### **2. What makes tasks meaningful**
Gamified systems should align game elements with the user’s *actual* needs, not arbitrary reward loops.

### **3. How to support autonomy and competence**
Using mechanics that support user choice, mastery, and exploration enhances intrinsic motivation.

### **4. How to integrate mechanics systemically**
Game elements work best when connected:

- badges alone do little  
- badges + challenges + feedback + goals → interactive system  

Gamification must **borrow from the structure of games**, not merely their surface symbols.

# CRITERIA FOR EVALUATING GAMIFICATION DESIGNS (PAGE 18)
The authors propose criteria to judge whether gamification is meaningful or superficial:

### **A. Coherence**
Do the game elements meaningfully align with the user’s goals and tasks?

### **B. Integration**
Are game elements woven into the system, not just bolted on?

### **C. Depth of design**
Do elements go beyond surface-level PBL additions?

### **D. Autonomy-support**
Does the system avoid coercion and preserve user freedom?

### **E. Competence support**
Does feedback promote a sense of progress and mastery?

### **F. Relatedness**
Do social or cooperative mechanics enhance user connection?

These criteria derive from both game studies and motivational psychology.

### **For industry**
Companies adopting gamification must understand that:

- superficial rewards produce inconsistent results  
- game design is a craft requiring expertise  
- gamification must match user motivations and context  
- deeper, systemic gamification is more effective than simple PBLs

### **For academia**
Researchers should:

- investigate user diversity and differential motivational profiles  

The field benefits from conceptual clarity, which this paper aims to advance.

# FUTURE DIRECTIONS FOR GAMIFICATION RESEARCH (PAGE 20)
The authors call for more rigorous, interdisciplinary research approaches to gamification. They emphasize that most early gamification deployments (2010–2011) lacked scientific evaluation. Future research should include:

### **1. Controlled empirical studies**
- Randomized controlled trials  
- Formal experiments examining motivational impact  
- Long-term retention and engagement outcomes  

### **2. Comparative analyses**
- Comparing different game design elements (e.g., points vs. quests)  
- Comparing extrinsic vs. intrinsic-oriented gamification  
- Examining how variations in game mechanics affect user behavior  

### **3. Cross-disciplinary integration**
Fields that should contribute:

- psychology (motivation, behavior)  
- design research  
- HCI (Human–Computer Interaction)  
- game studies  
- education  
- behavioral economics  
- sociology  

Gamification must not remain a marketing buzzword; it needs scientific grounding.

# USER EXPERIENCE AND GAMIFICATION (PAGE 21)

The authors stress that user experience (UX) must remain central to gamification design.

### **User Modeling**
Understanding users involves identifying:

- motivational profiles  
- skill levels  
- social preferences  
- susceptibility to extrinsic rewards  
- play styles (based on game studies taxonomies)

### **Personalization**
Gamified systems benefit from adapting:

- difficulty  
- rewards  
- pacing  
- social visibility  
- goals

Different users require different incentive structures.

### **Flow & Challenge Balance**
Gameful design should draw from Csikszentmihalyi’s concept of **Flow**:

- tasks must match the user’s skill level  
- too easy → boredom  
- too hard → anxiety  
- properly tuned → engagement  

Gamification frequently fails when it ignores the challenge-skill balance.

# RISKS AND ETHICS OF GAMIFICATION (PAGE 22)
The paper highlights several ethical issues that must be taken seriously.

### **1. Manipulation**
Gamification may nudge or push users toward actions they would not choose independently.

### **2. Exploitation**
Employers may use game elements to extract more productivity without proper compensation.

Examples:
- Mandatory leaderboards increasing work speed  
- Point systems pressuring workers into overtime  

### **3. Addiction & Overjustification**
Gamification may create:

- compulsive use loops  
- dependence on external rewards  
- diminishing intrinsic motivation  

### **4. Surveillance Concerns**
Gamified systems often require constant monitoring of user activity.

Potential issues:
- privacy  
- data collection  
- performance tracking  
- profiling without consent  

### **5. Inequality and Social Pressure**
Leaderboards may shame low performers or marginalize users who do not fit competitive norms.

Ethics must be part of responsible gamification design.
