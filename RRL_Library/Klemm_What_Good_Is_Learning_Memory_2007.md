# What Good Is Learning If You Don’t Remember It?  
**William R. Klemm**  
Texas A&M University, College Station, TX  

*The Journal of Effective Teaching, Vol. 7, No. 1, 2007, pp. 61–73*

---

## Abstract

Teachers should emphasize the educational importance of understanding, but not at the expense of overlooking the importance of memorization skills. Currently, mainstream educational theory embraces such attributes as insight, creativity, inquiry learning, and self-expression. But such emphases lead to a bias and under-appreciation of the role of memory in learning. Students cannot apply what they understand if they don’t remember it. Moreover, a good memory expands the repertoire of cognitive capabilities upon which new understandings can be developed and expedited. Effective thinking does not occur in a vacuum. I advocate adding another “R” to the “three Rs”: Reading, wRiting, aRithmetic, and Remembering. This paper attempts to show teachers how they can help students become better learners — and better thinkers — by improving their memorization skills.

**Keywords:** Learning; Memory; Memorization; Strategies; Attention; Association; Rehearsal; Recall

## Introduction

I have a faculty colleague who chastised me for writing a book for students on how to improve memory (Klemm, 2004). This colleague thinks that education should be all about understanding and using knowledge to solve problems. We need, he says, to teach students how to think. This colleague is like so many teachers these days who emphasize insight, creativity, inquiry learning, communication skills, and the like.

In science education, inquiry learning is all the rage (Layman, Ochoa, & Heikkinen, 1996; Martin, Sexton, & Franklin, 2004; Inquiry, 1999). In the process of educational reform, the reformers discount the importance of memory.  

I agree that the ultimate goal should be to teach people how to think, solve problems—and to create. Central to these capabilities, however, is the ability to remember things.

The more one knows (remembers), the more intellectual competencies one has to draw upon.

Knowledge and skills are acquired through memory.

Even our ability to think depends on memory. People think with their working memory, which is defined as small amounts of information accessible over short times that people can use in learning and thinking (Baddeley, 2000). I like to think of this memory metaphorically as “scratch pad” memory.

The thinking process is a successive operation in which successive “chunks” of scratch-pad memory are moved into the thinking process.

Thus, there is tight correlation between:

- IQ and working memory (Wickelgren, 2001)  
- and between working memory and problem-solving (Carpenter, Just, & Shell, 1990)

## What Good Is Understanding If You Don’t Remember?

This paper’s title is an explicit statement of my thesis.

Understanding information is certainly desirable, but what good is it if you don’t remember?

Examples:

- Many Ph.D.’s learned foreign languages they can no longer use.  
- Middle-school math students can solve problems only if they recall the steps.  

Memory is essential even in subjects requiring deep conceptual understanding.

---

## The Neglect of Memorization Training

Think back to your school days. How many teachers explicitly taught you **how to remember**?

Teachers might give acrostics or limericks or warn against cramming, but rarely provide systematic memory training.

Most students rely on **rote memorization**, which is:

- inefficient  
- encourages memorizing without thinking  
- ignores associations and strategies that make memory easier  

Durable memory is especially important in the era of high-stakes testing.

# Elements of Remembering

**Figure 1** (Page 3) shows the process of remembering.

The key components:

1. **Registration & Attention**  
2. **Association**  
3. **Rehearsal**  
4. **Consolidation**  
5. **Cueing & Recall**

### 1. Registration & Attention

To remember, information must register in the brain, which requires attention.

Poor memory in aging is often actually **poor attention** (Grady et al., 1995).

Attention aids encoding, and rehearsal strengthens memory.

### 2. Association

New information must “make contact” with the long-term knowledge store (Cowan, 1988).

Associations are essential for durable memory.

### 3. Rehearsal

Rehearsal keeps information in working memory and can begin consolidation.

Without rehearsal, new information easily evaporates.

### 4. Consolidation

Memory must be stabilized into long-term form. This process:

- takes time  
- is vulnerable to interference  
- depends on associations and salience cues  

### 5. Cueing & Recall

Recall relies on cues stored with the memory.

Blocked recall (“tip-of-the-tongue”) is often due to stress or the wrong cues.

**Figure 2** (Page 5) shows:

New information is:

- registered  
- compared with existing knowledge  
- assigned salience  
- reinforced through rehearsal  
- consolidated unless interference occurs  

Interference theory states that memories compete, and those temporally close interfere most heavily.

# Emotions and Memory

Emotion increases memorability.

- Positive attitude enhances memory  
- Negative attitude (e.g., hating a subject) impairs memory  
- Mood affects retrieval (Mineka & Nugent, 1995)  
- Depression impairs memory except for negative memories (Kizilbash et al., 2002)  

Stress harms both retrieval and neurons:

- test anxiety  
- chronic stress  
- cortisol toxicity in the hippocampus (Sapolsky, 1992; Lupien et al., 1998)

---

# Sleep and Memory

Sleep is **necessary** for full consolidation:

- deep sleep supports declarative memory (Plihal & Born, 1997)  
- REM/dream sleep supports procedural and perceptual learning (Stickgold et al., 2000)

Most adolescents are chronically sleep-deprived, impairing learning.

# Specific Memorization Strategies

### 1. Attention

Multitasking severely degrades memory (de Fockert et al., 2001; Foerde et al., 2006).

### 2. Organization

Memory improves when:

- material is categorized  
- associations are grouped  
- patterns are connected  

### 3. Association

Visual imagery is especially powerful:

- brains devote vast resources to visual processing  
- vivid, bizarre images are more memorable  
- linking images into stories enhances recall  

Context and location cues also help (Godden & Baddeley, 1975).

### 4. Chunking

Working memory capacity is ≈ 4 items (Cowan, 2005).

Chunking reduces cognitive load.

### 5. Rehearsal

Rehearsal must occur:

- soon after learning  
- repeatedly  
- without interference  

Cramming leads only to short-term memory.

Multiple-choice tests assess recognition, not retrieval, and overestimate competence.

---

# A Teaching Game Plan That Works

**The 10-Minute Rule**

1. Teach/learn new material for **10 minutes**  
2. Rehearse and apply it for **10 minutes**  
3. Take a **short break** without new information  
4. Repeat with the next chunk

This structure maximizes:

- attention  
- rehearsal  
- consolidation  
- association formation  
- long-term retention  

---

# Conclusions

Many students underachieve because they have never been taught **how to memorize**.

Teachers should:

- allocate time to train memory strategies  
- explain variables that affect memory  
- reduce reliance on rote memorization  
- integrate rehearsal, visualization, associations  

Memory is essential for thinking, problem-solving, creativity, and skill acquisition.
